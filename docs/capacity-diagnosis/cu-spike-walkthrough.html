Anatomy of a CU spike: diagnosing a runaway semantic model

A deliberate high-CU event was generated on a live capacity, then traced back to its cause using the Capacity Metrics App and cross-checked in FUAM. Everything below is a real screenshot — capacity Trial-Central64 (FTL64), tenant MngEnvMCAP777813, 2026-09-02.

Peak utilization 204.65% Interactive at one timepoint 201.74% Carryforward 419% Time to diagnose 5 clicks

What was done, and why

To produce a spike that could be traced honestly, 564 unique DAX queries were fired at one semantic model — FUAM_Core_SM — over about twelve minutes, in four escalating bursts:

EVALUATE ROW("x", SUMX(GENERATESERIES(1, 25000000), SQRT([Value]) * SIN([Value])))

Two details matter. Each query is a pure CPU iteration, so the cost lands on the model rather than on storage. And each query is made unique — the first attempt reused an identical query and the engine returned a cached result in 2 s instead of 9 s, producing almost no CU. A repeated identical query is not a load test.

The run also hit a documented limit worth knowing: the Power BI executeQueries REST API is capped at 120 requests per minute per user. One burst of 240 returned exactly 120 successes and 120 HTTP 429s.

Step 1 — Notice the spike

1
Health page, switched to Last 1 hour. The 24-hour default hides a short event by averaging it away. On the one-hour view the capacity reads 26.50% average against an 8.01% seven-day baseline — more than three times normal. P95 interactive delay is 72.00, meaning the capacity spent part of the hour at 72% of the threshold where interactive requests start being delayed.
Read the health row, not just the colour. Health still says Healthy and rejected and failed counts are both zero. The signal is in the comparison — average utilization against the seven-day figure, and the P95 columns. A capacity can be three times its normal load and still be labelled healthy.

Step 2 — Confirm it on the Compute page

2
Peak utilization 204.65%. The utilization chart shows a single narrow column crossing the dotted CU % Limit line at the right-hand edge. Everything before it sits under 20%. This is the shape of a burst, not of growth — a capacity that is genuinely undersized rises gradually across many days.
3
Check the throttling tab before assuming impact. The Interactive delay view shows the 10-minute smoothed percentage rising to roughly 90% of its threshold and stopping short of it. Exceeding 100% of base capacity is not the same as being throttled: bursting absorbed this. Had the burst continued another minute or two, users would have started seeing delays.

Step 3 — Narrow to the day and read the item list

4
Filter the ribbon chart to the day. This is the step that is easy to miss. Selecting a date on the ribbon chart does two things: the utilization chart drops from a six-minute peak rollup to every 30-second timepoint, and the items table switches from a 14-day total to that day only. The offender is now unmistakable — FUAM_Core_SM at 58,588 CU(s), three times the next item.

The diagnosis. A single semantic model, FUAM_Core_SM, served 152 concurrent Query operations in one 30-second window, all from one user. The most expensive single query consumed 2,800 CU(s) and claimed 14.58% of the entire capacity on its own. Ten queries were enough to exceed base capacity twice over. Nothing failed and nothing was rejected — the cost is entirely in concurrency, not in errors.
The burndown table is the part people skip. It shows AS (Analysis Services) adding 102.44% of carryforward in this window, with cumulative carryforward at 419.18% and 2.1 minutes to burn down. That is the real exposure: the capacity has committed the next two minutes of its budget to work already accepted. Another burst inside that window is what turns a spike into throttling.

The same event in FUAM

6
FUAM saw it — by eleven minutes. FUAM's pipeline runs daily at 05:00 UTC. The burst ended at 04:49 UTC, so the run happened to fall just after it, and the Metrics App data had landed in time to be extracted. FUAM recorded 58,348.8 CU(s) for Dataset / Query against the Metrics App's 58,588.4 — a 0.4% difference, because FUAM read while the last few minutes were still arriving.
That timing was luck, and it is the point. Had the burst happened at 06:00 UTC instead, FUAM would not have shown it until the following day's run — roughly 23 hours later. The Metrics App had it in about 13 minutes. For is something wrong right now, scheduled extraction is the wrong instrument no matter how good the reports are.

What to take from this. The Metrics App answered the whole question in five clicks, and only because the item list was filtered to a single day first — on the 14-day total this model sat fourth and looked unremarkable. FUAM independently confirmed the number, which is a useful check on an extract that is otherwise unsupported. Neither tool would have told anyone at the time: that requires Fabric capacity overview events and an Activator rule.
